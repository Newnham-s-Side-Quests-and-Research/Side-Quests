Custom autonomous drone

Equations, worked examples and the assumptions used in the software reconstruction.

Evidence boundary

The original firmware, component records and flight measurements are unavailable. Values described as assumptions define a repeatable simulation. They are not measurements of the original aircraft or proof of physical flight performance. The rigid body model is separate from the dashboard playback.

1. Coordinate conventions

The rigid body example uses a right handed world frame with the vertical axis pointing up. Its body frame also points up when level. Position and velocity are world frame quantities. Angular velocity and rotor positions use body axes. The quaternion maps body directions into the world frame and is recorded in scalar first order.

m
Aircraft mass in kilograms
g
Gravitational acceleration in metres per second squared
Ti
Upward body axis thrust from rotor i in newtons
v,vw
Aircraft velocity and wind velocity in metres per second
c
Assumed linear drag coefficient in newton seconds per metre
q
Unit quaternion mapping body axes to world axes

2. Translational motion

Each rotor applies its thrust along the positive body vertical axis. The attitude rotates the combined thrust into the world frame. Gravity acts down and the simple drag term opposes velocity relative to the wind.

ma=R(q)[0,0,∑i=14Ti]T−mgez−c(v−vw)
(1)

Here R(q) is the body to world rotation and ez is the world upward unit vector. The drag model is an illustration and is not calibrated aerodynamic data.

Worked example: level hover

The fixture assumes a mass of 1 kg, gravity of 9.81 m/s² and four equal thrusts of 2.4525 N. At rest in still air the total thrust is 4 × 2.4525 = 9.81 N. Weight is 1 × 9.81 = 9.81 N. The net vertical force is zero and the initial vertical acceleration is 0 m/s². These values are model inputs rather than measurements.

3. Rotational motion

Rotor position is measured from the assumed centre of mass. The first two moment components come from the cross product of rotor position and thrust. The third is an assumed reaction moment. A diagonal inertia tensor is used.

τ=∑i=14(ri×Fi)+k∑i=14siTiez
(2)
ω˙=I−1[τ−ω×(Iω)]
(3)

ri is the rotor position and si is its assigned reaction moment sign. k is the assumed reaction moment per unit thrust. I is the body inertia tensor and ω is body angular velocity.

Worked example: one motor loses command

At the first step all four motors produce 2.4525 N. For an assumed time constant of 0.05 s and a step of 0.01 s, equation (4) gives motor 1 a thrust of approximately 2.00794 N after its command becomes zero. Its decrease is approximately 0.44456 N. The assumed 0.075 m lateral arm gives a roll moment of approximately −0.03334 N m at the next sample. The change also creates pitch and yaw moments. It does not establish how the original motor or aircraft behaved.

4. Motor response and time steps

The motor model assumes a constant commanded thrust during each step. It applies a first order response to that command.

Tn+1=Tn+(1−e−Δt/τ)(Tcommand−Tn)
(4)

τ is the assumed motor response time in seconds and Δt is the simulation step. Position, velocity and angular velocity use explicit Euler updates. The quaternion is updated from body angular velocity and normalised. Ground contact ends the run at an interpolated position without modelling impact.

xn+1=xn+vnΔtvn+1=vn+anΔt
(5)

5. Three cell battery example

The electrical example is separate from the flight models. It assumes three standard lithium polymer cells in series and uses 3.7 V per cell as a nominal reference and 4.2 V per cell as a full charge reference. Actual charging and operating limits depend on the selected pack and its manufacturer.

Vpack=NVcell
(6)

Worked example: voltage and power

Nominal pack voltage = 3 × 3.7 = 11.1 V. Full charge reference = 3 × 4.2 = 12.6 V. At an assumed constant 8 A and 11.1 V, electrical power = 11.1 × 8 = 88.8 W. Current and capacity in the dashboard are assumptions rather than measurements. The example does not predict voltage sag or runtime under real motor load.

6. Position control example

A separate controlled simulation requests acceleration from position error and velocity. The calculation runs either in Python or in the optional C module. Horizontal position and velocity are ideal model values. Vertical position and velocity come from the pressure based filter. The target is held fixed during this example.

ai,request=kp,i(xi,target−x^i)−kd,iv^i
(7)

For axis i, the target and estimated position use metres and the estimated velocity uses metres per second. The proportional gain has units of inverse seconds squared. The derivative gain has units of inverse seconds. The requested acceleration uses metres per second squared. The controller then calculates a limited desired tilt and motor commands. The gains are assumptions for this simulation.

7. Verification and limits

Automated checks compare the implementation with independently specified cases for balanced hover, a rotated thrust direction, motor response, rotor moments, constant rotation and free fall. The checks cover those cases rather than all possible behaviour. The controlled example does not include battery coupling or measured motor and propeller data. It does not establish real flight performance.

The mathematical form of rigid body rotational acceleration is consistent with the model described in the UAV Toolbox documentation. The implementation here uses its own stated axes and simplified forces.
